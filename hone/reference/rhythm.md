# Rhythm, em dashes, and the register band

Prose can clear every defect in `defects.md` and still read as machine-written, because cadence is
not a defect you can name in a sentence — it is a property of the paragraph. This file holds the
positive guidance: what good rhythm sounds like, how to diagnose em-dash density, and how far the
confidence dial may travel before it reads as either timid or boastful.

---

## Writing rhythm (mandatory)

All content has dynamic sentence-length variation. Mix short 3-5 word sentences with
longer complex thoughts. Completely eliminate paragraphs with a uniform rhythm that
looks AI-generated. Controlled variation, not randomness.

- Vary sentence length deliberately; short sentences carry emphasis, long sentences
  carry reasoning. Use fragments selectively. For punch.
- Vary paragraph length and sentence openings; let cadence follow meaning.
- Read important copy aloud; if the rhythm is metronomic, rewrite.
- Never: five equally structured sentences in a row, repeated subject-verb-benefit
  construction, decorative rule-of-three, manufactured punchiness.

**Worked example.** Uniform (rewrite this): "DataJoint codifies experiments as data.
DataJoint makes workflows deterministic. DataJoint keeps provenance queryable. DataJoint
helps teams move faster." Varied (ship this): "Experiments are modeled, not stored. That
one decision changes everything downstream: workflows become deterministic, provenance
becomes queryable, and the platforms you already run finally get inputs they can trust.
The work compounds."

## Em dashes

**Permitted on every surface, per AP: a genuine interruption, spaced (` — `).** The mark
is not a defect and never has been; the 2022 guide specified it, our documentation carries
1,618 of them, and it does work no other mark does — an aside that cuts across the
sentence, where a colon would point forward and commas would blur into the clauses
already there.

**Why it gets overused.** The em dash is the low-commitment mark. Every other connector
forces a decision about the relationship between two clauses — `because` claims cause,
`which` claims reference, `namely` claims restatement, a colon claims that what follows
explains what precedes. The dash claims nothing. It attaches material while leaving the
relation unstated, which is exactly what a writer reaches for when the relation has not
been worked out. That is why generated drafts are dense with it: fluent continuation is
cheap, and the dash always fits. **The overuse is a symptom of unresolved logic, not of
punctuation, which is why the fix is to name the relation rather than to swap the mark.**

**The overuse patterns, in the order they show up in our drafts:**

| Pattern | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Undecided connector** | The dash stands in for a relation the sentence never states | Name it: *because*, *which*, *namely*, or a colon. If none fits, the clause is decoration |
| **Two in one sentence** | Nested asides; the reader loses the main clause | One aside per sentence. See **Appositive pile-up** |
| **Appended afterthought** | A complete sentence, then a dash and a trailing benefit or restatement — *"and that changes everything"* | Cut it, or make it a sentence that carries a claim. Sibling of defect 2 |
| **Staged reveal** | The dash used for drama rather than interruption: *"and that is the point — the schema is the lineage"* | State the point. Emphasis by punctuation is emphasis unearned |
| **Default apposition** | Every aside on the page set with a dash, whatever its strength | A mild aside takes commas; an introduction takes a colon. Reserve the dash for a real interruption |
| **Frequency signature** | One dash in every paragraph, or more dashes on a page than paragraphs | Not any single sentence's fault, and the clearest tell of the lot. Rewrite half of them into stated relations |

**Fixed copy is the one absolute:** never in a locked statement, tagline, headline, or
eyebrow. Those are short, scanned, and carry no subordination at all.

None of this is a count. A page with four em dashes, each marking a genuine interruption,
is well punctuated; a page with four where three stand in for unresolved logic needs the
logic, not fewer dashes.

## The speech test

**Read the sentence aloud as though saying it to one person. If you would never say it, it does not
belong in the piece, however well it reads on the page.**

The failure has a signature: an abstract noun handed a human or physical verb, in the cadence of a
magazine essay. *A quieter question arrives a few months in.* *A congress forms when a field moves
faster than its practitioners can compare notes.* *Doubt creeps in.* *A tension runs through the
program.* Each is grammatical, none is false, and no author says any of them out loud — which is why
a piece built from them stops sounding like the person whose name is on it. The reader does not
diagnose it; they just stop hearing a voice.

Two places it enters:

- **Adapting a talk to prose.** The spoken original is already in the author's voice and usually
  needs shortening, not elevating. A written version that improves on speech generally only
  literaturizes it. Mine the script first; where the essay and the script disagree, the script is
  the evidence of how the author actually speaks.
- **Openings.** Scene-setting before the claim is where the borrowed cadence is most tempting, and
  it compounds with **Throat-clearing opener** (defect 10). An opening earns its place by making
  the claim, not by setting a scene.

The fix is the words you would use: name who is doing the thing (*"a few months in, people start
asking whether any of it compounds"*), or drop the frame and ask the question. The frame usually
deletes cleanly, which is **Cut what survives cutting** (principle 1); what is left under it was
the sentence you meant.

**This is not a ban on figures of speech.** A metaphor the author would say aloud stays — the test
is speech, not plainness. *"If we think of models as wild horses"* is spoken English and survives;
*"a quieter question arrives"* is not, and does not.
